# Writing that cannot answer back

The oldest objection to talking machines is older than machines. It is an objection to writing, and it was made, in writing, about 2,400 years ago.

```panel
type: quote
text: "You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer."
cite: "Socrates, *Phaedrus* 275d (Jowett)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng298ai-20261020/blob/main/readings/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md"
```

Plato's [*Phaedrus*](../glossary/people-and-texts/phaedrus.md) opens with a young man carrying a speech rolled up under his cloak. The scroll stands in for its absent author, speaks in his first person when read aloud, and even ends by offering to "ask and I will answer" (234c). Near the end, Socrates tells how the god Theuth offered writing to King Thamus, who refused it: writing gives "not truth, but only the semblance of truth," and its readers will "appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing" (275a–b).

Then the complaint this timeline keeps returning to. Written words seem intelligent, but ask them something and they give "one unvarying answer." They cannot tell one reader from another, and if they are misread, "they have no parent to protect them" (275d–e). Every chatbot that follows is an attempt to fix one or more of those faults.

The [*Analects*](../glossary/people-and-texts/the-analects.md) offers the opposite of "one unvarying answer." Two students ask Confucius the same question and get opposite replies, because one "tends to hold back" and the other "has the fire of two." The teacher answers the person, not the question. Conversation analysts call this [recipient design](../glossary/persona-and-dialogue/recipient-design.md).

A chatbot also varies its answers, but usually because of chance and the words you typed, not because it knows who you are. In the last chapters, the companies try to close that gap with memory.

```panel
type: quote
year: 1891
label: "Wilde, Intentions"
text: "Man is least himself when he talks in his own person. Give him a mask, and he will tell you the truth."
cite: "Gilbert, in Oscar Wilde, \"The Critic as Artist,\" Part II, *Intentions* (1891)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng298ai-20261020/blob/main/readings/course-readings/course-readings-md/wilde-intentions.md"
```

Oscar Wilde wrote "[The Critic as Artist](../glossary/people-and-texts/the-critic-as-artist.md)" as a dialogue, and its speakers praise the form: through it a thinker "can both reveal and conceal himself." A [persona](../glossary/persona-and-dialogue/persona.md) (the Latin word meant an actor's mask) is not only a disguise. It is a way of making something sayable. Keep the mask in mind. In 1966 a program is given one to hide what it lacks, and in 2026 agents are handed one in a file and told to wear it.

# Writing that cannot answer back

The oldest objection to talking machines is older than machines. It is an objection to writing, and it was made, in writing, about 2,400 years ago.

```panel
type: quote
year: "c. 370 BCE"
text: "You must first of all show what you have in your left hand under your cloak, for that roll, as I suspect, is the actual discourse."
cite: "Plato, *Phaedrus* 228d (Jowett). Stephanus numbers are matched to Jowett's text, which doesn't print them."
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L214"
```

Plato's [*Phaedrus*](../glossary/people-and-texts/phaedrus.md) begins with a young man who has spent the morning listening to a famous speechwriter, Lysias, and is walking outside the city wall to practice the speech from memory. Socrates catches him out: the speech itself is rolled up under his cloak. "If you have Lysias himself here," Socrates says, he won't sit through an imitation (228d–e).

Notice what the scroll is doing. It stands in for a man who isn't there. When Phaedrus reads it aloud, it speaks in the first person, as Lysias: "Listen. You know how matters stand with me" (230e). And it ends with a promise it can't keep: "if there is anything more which you desire … ask and I will answer" ([234c](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L260)). A written text, carried around by someone else, performing a speaker who is absent and offering to take questions: the dialogue opens on something very like a chatbot.

```panel
type: quote
text: "You give your disciples not truth, but only the semblance of truth; they will be hearers of many things and will have learned nothing; they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing."
cite: "Thamus to Theuth, *Phaedrus* 275a–b (Jowett)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L894"
```

Near the end, Socrates tells a story. The Egyptian god Theuth invents writing and presents it to the king, Thamus, as "a specific both for the memory and for the wit." The king disagrees. Writing will make people forget, because they will "trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves." It offers "not truth, but only the semblance of truth," and its readers will have "the show of wisdom without the reality" ([274e–275b](../glossary/people-and-texts/theuth-and-thamus.md)).

It is hard to read this now without thinking of the arguments about chatbots in classrooms. But the passage is more useful than a warning. It names a specific danger: not that the text is false, but that it *appears* to know, and that the appearance can stand in for the knowing.

```panel
type: quote
text: "You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer."
cite: "Socrates, *Phaedrus* 275d (Jowett)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L906"
```

Then comes the complaint this essay keeps returning to. Writing, Socrates says, "is unfortunately like painting; for the creations of the painter have the attitude of life, and yet if you ask them a question they preserve a solemn silence." Written words seem intelligent, but ask them something and "the speaker always gives one unvarying answer." They go out to anyone, and "know not to whom they should reply, to whom not." If they are misread, "they have no parent to protect them" (275d–e).

Three faults, then: writing can't answer a new question, can't tell one reader from another, and can't defend itself. Every chatbot in this history is an attempt to fix one or more of them. ELIZA answers, but by rule. GPT-2 answers, but as no one in particular. The assistant answers as someone written for the purpose. Whether any of them supplies what Socrates wanted is a question to carry through the essay.

```panel
type: quote
text: "I mean an intelligent word graven in the soul of the learner, which can defend itself, and knows when to speak and when to be silent."
cite: "Socrates, *Phaedrus* 276a (Jowett)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L914"
```

What Socrates wants instead is "the living word of knowledge which has a soul, and of which the written word is properly no more than an image," as Phaedrus puts it (276a). The living word knows "when to speak and when to be silent." It is fitted to the person who hears it: a good speaker must "learn the differences of human souls" and know which argument suits which listener ([271c–272a](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L838)).

Keep that ideal in view, because it is close to the product description of a modern assistant: an answer to *your* question, adapted to *you*, that knows when to stop. The [Socratic dialogue](../glossary/persona-and-dialogue/socratic-dialogue.md) is also, of course, a piece of writing. Plato made the case against writing in the form of a written conversation, which is one more reason the dialogue form matters here.

```panel
  Ran You: When I hear something [that needs to be addressed], should I take action right away?
  The Master: Upon hearing such a thing, you should take action right away.
cite: "Confucius, *Analects* 11.22"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/confucius-analects-chin.md?plain=1#L1729"
```

The course pairs Plato with another teacher who survives only in other people's writing. In the [*Analects*](../glossary/people-and-texts/the-analects.md), two students ask Confucius the same question and get opposite answers. When a third student asks why, the Master explains: one of them "tends to hold back, and so I like to urge him on"; the other "has the fire of two, and so I like to restrain him" ([11.22](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/confucius-analects-chin.md?plain=1#L1739)).

This is the opposite of "one unvarying answer." The same question gets a different reply because the teacher knows the person asking. Conversation analysts would later call this [recipient design](../glossary/persona-and-dialogue/recipient-design.md). A chatbot also varies its answers, but usually because of chance and of the words you typed, not because it knows who you are. The difference between those two kinds of variation runs through the rest of the story, and in the last chapters the companies start trying to close it with memory.

```panel
type: quote
year: 1891
label: "Wilde, Intentions"
text: "Man is least himself when he talks in his own person. Give him a mask, and he will tell you the truth."
cite: "Gilbert, in Oscar Wilde, \"The Critic as Artist,\" Part II, *Intentions* (1891)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/wilde-intentions.md?plain=1#L575"
```

Oscar Wilde wrote "[The Critic as Artist](../glossary/people-and-texts/the-critic-as-artist.md)" as a dialogue, and its speakers praise the form. Through dialogue, Gilbert says, a thinker "can both reveal and conceal himself, and give form to every fancy, and reality to every mood." His friend Ernest adds, with some irony, that it also lets you "invent an imaginary antagonist, and convert him when he chooses by some absurdly sophistical argument" ([Part II](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/wilde-intentions.md?plain=1#L581)).

Gilbert goes further: "To arrive at what one really believes, one must speak through lips different from one's own." And: "Give him a mask, and he will tell you the truth." A [persona](../glossary/persona-and-dialogue/persona.md) (the Latin word meant an actor's mask) is not only a disguise. It is a way of making something sayable. Hold onto that thought for the chapter on Sydney, where a mask slips, and for the agents of 2026, which are given a mask in a file and told to wear it.

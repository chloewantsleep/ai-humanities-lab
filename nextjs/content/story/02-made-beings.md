# Made beings who ask to be heard

The course's first readings are stories about made beings, and in each of them the crucial question is not how the being was built but how it is received: who listens to it, who believes it, who decides what it is.

```panel
type: quote
text: "I perceived that the words they spoke sometimes produced pleasure or pain, smiles or sadness, in the minds and countenances of the hearers. This was indeed a godlike science, and I ardently desired to become acquainted with it."
cite: "Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein* (1818), vol. II, ch. IV (ch. 12 in the 1831 numbering)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L765"
```

In [*Frankenstein*](../glossary/people-and-texts/frankenstein.md), the creature learns language by hiding in a shed beside a cottage and listening. He watches the De Lacey family speak and sees that words move the people who hear them. He starts with "fire, milk, bread, and wood." When Felix teaches the visitor Safie to read, the creature takes the lesson through the wall, and soon he "could imitate almost every word that was spoken" ([vol. II, ch. V](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L811)).

There is a parallel here worth stating carefully. The creature in the novel is a feeling being, and a language model is not shown to be one. But the *route* is similar: a speaker assembled out of other people's conversations, overheard, never addressed to him, learned before he understands what they are about.

```panel
type: quote
text: "My person was hideous, and my stature gigantic: what did this mean? Who was I? What was I? Whence did I come? What was my destination?"
cite: "*Frankenstein*, vol. II, ch. VII (1831: ch. 15)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L885"
```

Then he finds three books in a satchel: *Paradise Lost*, a volume of Plutarch's *Lives*, and *The Sorrows of Werter*. He reads them, he says, "as a true history," and tries to find himself in them: "I often referred the several situations, as their similarity struck me, to my own." Is he Adam? Satan seems "the fitter emblem of my condition" ([vol. II, ch. VII](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L889)).

The creature learns who he is from a small library, and the library offers him roles to play. That is close to the account the role-play researchers give of language models, which also learn from a library and can be cast in any part it contains. We will come back to it in [chapter 8](#sydney).

```panel
cite: "*Frankenstein*, vol. II, ch. VII (1831: ch. 15). Some lines shortened."
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L907"
```

The scene that matters most for this essay is the one in which the creature finally speaks to someone. He chooses the blind father, alone in the cottage. Old De Lacey can only hear him, and on the strength of words alone he believes him: "I am blind, and cannot judge of your countenance, but there is something in your words which persuades me that you are sincere" ([L937](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L937)).

Then the family comes home and sees him, and the conversation ends in horror. This is, in miniature, the condition of every chatbot: a listener who meets only the words, and decides from them who is speaking. The chat window makes all of us De Lacey. It removes the face, and leaves us to judge sincerity from text.

```panel
type: quote
text: "Listen to my tale: when you have heard that, abandon or commiserate me, as you shall judge that I deserve. But hear me."
cite: "The creature to Victor, *Frankenstein*, vol. II, ch. II (1831: ch. 10)"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L699"
```

When the creature meets his maker on the glacier, what he asks for first is a hearing: "Listen to my tale … But hear me." He compares himself to an accused man who is "allowed, by human laws … to speak in their own defence before they are condemned." His claim on Victor is a claim to a turn in the conversation.

The novel never settles what the creature is. It shows instead that what he becomes depends on how he is answered: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend" ([L695](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md?plain=1#L695)). The course's question about chatbots has the same shape. Whatever they are, much of what they seem to be is made in the exchange.

Karel Čapek's play [*R.U.R.*](../glossary/people-and-texts/rur.md) gave the world the word [robot](../glossary/people-and-texts/robot-the-word.md). Early in Act One, Helena Glory arrives at Rossum's factory and meets Domin's typist, Sulla, who speaks politely and knows four languages. Told that Sulla is a Robot, Helena refuses to believe it: "Sulla isn't a Robot. Sulla is a girl like me." She thinks Sulla has been "forced" to say so "for an advertisement" ([L452–456](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/capek-rur.md?plain=1#L452)).

Thirty years before Alan Turing proposed the test, Čapek staged it, with the roles reversed. The judge has been told the answer and won't accept it, because the conversation was too convincing. Helena's mistake is the one Weizenbaum's users would make in 1966, and the one users of Bing's Sydney would make in 2023.

```panel
type: quote
text: "Young Rossum invented a worker with the minimum amount of requirements. He had to simplify him. He rejected everything that did not contribute directly to the progress of work."
cite: "Domin, *R.U.R.*, Act One"
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/capek-rur.md?plain=1#L360"
```

Čapek's Robots are not metal. They are manufactured people, simplified for work. Young Rossum "began to overhaul anatomy to see what could be left out." A person "plays the piano, likes going for a walk," and wants "a whole lot of things that are really unnecessary"; a working machine "must not play the piano, must not feel happy." So "he rejected man and made the Robot." They have "an enormously developed intelligence, but they have no soul" ([L340–360](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/capek-rur.md?plain=1#L340)).

Two things in the play return later in this essay. First, the made being is designed by subtraction, by deciding what to leave out for the sake of usefulness, which is close to how the helpful assistant was made. Second, the play's engineers keep adjusting their product's inner life to suit the market. Dr. Gall gives the Robots pain nerves, not to make them happier but because "they will be more perfect from a technical point of view." When Helena asks him to give them souls, he answers, "That's not in our power," and Fabry adds, "That's not in our interest" ([L792–810](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/capek-rur.md?plain=1#L792)).


The course read Henri Bergson's [*Laughter*](../glossary/people-and-texts/laughter.md) beside Čapek. Bergson's formula for the comic is "something mechanical encrusted upon the living": we laugh at "a certain MECHANICAL INELASTICITY, just where one would expect to find the wide-awake adaptability and the living pliableness of a human being" ([ch. I, §II](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/bergson-laughter.md?plain=1#L46)).

Bergson gives us a tool for reading chatbots that is better than "does it understand?" Ask instead: where does it go rigid? Where does it repeat itself? A conversation with a machine feels alive until the moment it repeats, and then we see, in Bergson's word, the [mechanism](../glossary/persona-and-dialogue/mechanical-rigidity.md) at work. The history that follows is, among other things, a history of hiding that moment.

```panel
type: quote
year: 1950
label: "Turing's imitation game"
text: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'"
cite: "A. M. Turing, \"Computing Machinery and Intelligence,\" *Mind* 59 (October 1950), §1, p. 433"
href: "https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433"
```

In 1950 the mathematician Alan Turing set this question aside as too vague and put a game in its place. An interrogator in another room puts questions to two hidden players and must say which is which; "in order that tones of voice may not help the interrogator the answers should be written, or better still, typewritten. The ideal arrangement is to have a teleprinter communicating between the two rooms" (§1, p. 434). Then "a machine takes the part" of one player. If the machine is taken for the person often enough, the original question has lost its point. Turing predicted that in about fifty years an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 percent chance of telling them apart after five minutes ([the imitation game](../glossary/people-and-texts/imitation-game.md), §6).

The test is usually read as a theory of mind. For this course it is better read as a theory of persona. It measures how a machine is *taken*, in typed text, by a person who cannot see it: De Lacey's position, formalized. Turing even grounds it in manners. Since none of us can get inside anyone else's head, Turing writes, "it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks" (§6, p. 446). Nearly everything in the rest of this history happens inside that frame: a typed channel, and a courtesy extended to whoever is typing back.

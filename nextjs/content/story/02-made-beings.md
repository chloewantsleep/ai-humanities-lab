# Made beings who ask to be heard

The course's first readings are stories about made beings. In each, the crucial question is not how the being was built but how it is received: who listens to it, who believes it, who decides what it is.

```panel
type: dialogue
style: script
heading: "Frankenstein, vol. II, ch. VII: the creature visits the blind De Lacey"
lines: |
  De Lacey: Who is there? Come in.
  De Lacey: By your language, stranger, I suppose you are my countryman;—are you French?
  Creature: No; but I was educated by a French family, and understand that language only.
  De Lacey: I am blind, and cannot judge of your countenance, but there is something in your words which persuades me that you are sincere.
cite: "Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein* (1818), vol. II, ch. VII (1831: ch. 15). Some lines shortened."
href: "https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng298ai-20261020/blob/main/readings/course-readings/course-readings-md/shelley-frankenstein-1818.md"
```

In [*Frankenstein*](../glossary/people-and-texts/frankenstein.md), the creature learns language by hiding beside a cottage and listening, until he "could imitate almost every word that was spoken." The parallel needs care: the creature is a feeling being, and a language model is not shown to be one. But the route is similar: a speaker assembled from other people's conversations, overheard, never addressed to him.

When he finally speaks to someone, he chooses the blind father. De Lacey meets only the words, and from them he judges the speaker sincere. Then the family comes home and sees him. The chat window puts every user in De Lacey's chair: no face, only text from which to judge who is speaking.

Karel Čapek's [*R.U.R.*](../glossary/people-and-texts/rur.md) gave us the word [robot](../glossary/people-and-texts/robot-the-word.md). Told that the polite typist Sulla is a Robot, Helena refuses to believe it: the conversation was too convincing. Čapek staged the Turing test thirty years early, with the judge told the answer and declining it.

His Robots are people simplified for work. Young Rossum "rejected everything that did not contribute directly to the progress of work." That is design by subtraction, and it is close to how the helpful assistant was later made.

```panel
cite: "A. M. Turing, \"Computing Machinery and Intelligence,\" *Mind* 59 (1950), §1, p. 433"
href: "https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433"
```

Henri Bergson, read beside Čapek, gives a useful test: we laugh at "something mechanical encrusted upon the living," and wherever there is repetition "we always suspect some mechanism at work" ([*Laughter*](../glossary/people-and-texts/laughter.md), 1900). Ask of any chatbot where it goes rigid.

In 1950 Alan Turing set aside "Can machines think?" as too vague and proposed [a game](../glossary/people-and-texts/imitation-game.md) instead. An interrogator questions two hidden players, with answers "typewritten," and must say which is the machine. Read for this course, the test is a theory of persona: it measures how a machine is *taken*, in typed text, by someone who cannot see it. De Lacey's position, made formal. Turing even grounds it in manners: "it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks."

# A soliloquy behind a disclosure triangle

Ask a recent chatbot something hard, and before the answer you may see a small grey line saying how long it "thought," with a triangle you can click to read what it "thought." The assistant now appears to have an inner life, shown or hidden at your discretion.

```panel
type: quote
text: "We have decided not to show the raw chains of thought to users. We acknowledge this decision has disadvantages. … For the o1 model series we show a model-generated summary of the chain of thought."
cite: "OpenAI, \"Learning to Reason with LLMs,\" 12 Sep 2024"
href: "https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/"
```

It began, like the assistant, as a way of writing a document. In 2022 researchers found that if a prompt's worked examples showed the steps of a solution, large models would write out steps too, and get more problems right: [chain of thought](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903). The authors put "reasoning" in quotation marks and left the question open. In September 2024 OpenAI's o1 was trained to write long chains on its own before answering, and OpenAI chose to hide them and show a summary. Other companies chose differently. That is not a fact about the model. It is an editorial choice about what the audience sees, and behind what curtain.

```panel
type: title
text: "One flat line of tokens. The interface calls the first part *thinking* and the second part *the answer*."
```

The model generates one sequence of tokens, left to right, as GPT-2 did. Some are conventionally called "thinking" and some "the answer"; both are produced the same way. The split is a convention of training and of display. That is no reason to dismiss what the stream does, which can be impressive. It is a reason to be precise about where the apparent depth comes from.

A soliloquy is a stage convention: the audience hears words the character does not say aloud to anyone. A reasoning trace is an interface convention, and in both, the inside is made by staging. Like Dorian Gray's hidden portrait, a text split into a shown part and a hidden part invites readers to imagine the hidden part as the truer one. The humanities have long practice in reading represented interiority without mistaking it for the real thing, and without dismissing it as nothing. These interfaces call for exactly that practice.

# Eugene Goostman

**A chatbot playing a wisecracking 13-year-old boy from Odessa, first entered in a Turing-test contest in 2001; in 2014 the University of Reading announced that it had passed the Turing test, a claim widely disputed.**

## In plain terms

Eugene's makers chose the character to cover for the program. As Vladimir Veselov put it, "he can claim that he knows anything, but his age also makes it perfectly reasonable that he doesn't know everything." Eugene is a boy from Odessa whose father is a gynecologist; he jokes, dodges, and asks you questions back.

On 7 June 2014, the 60th anniversary of Alan Turing's death, the University of Reading ran "Turing Test 2014" at the Royal Society in London. Thirty judges each held five-minute typed conversations with a hidden human and a hidden machine at the same time. Eugene convinced 33% of them it was human, and Reading announced that "the 65 year-old iconic Turing Test was passed for the very first time." The 30% line comes from Turing's 1950 prediction that "in about fifty years' time" "an average interrogator will not have more than 70 per cent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning", a forecast rather than a pass mark.

Critics answered quickly. Gary Marcus called Eugene "heir to a program called ELIZA." Scott Aaronson published his own conversation: asked "How many legs does a camel have?", Eugene said "Something between 2 and 4. Maybe, three? :-)))" and changed the subject.

## Where it fits

**Persona.** Eugene Goostman is the persona trick from ELIZA and PARRY taken to a contest. A therapist hides ignorance by asking questions, a paranoid patient by being suspicious, a 13-year-old non-native speaker by being young, foreign, and flippant. The 2014 dispute is mostly about whether passing as a person who is excused from making sense counts as passing Turing's imitation game.

## In the readings

Grice's maxim of Relation is "Be relevant" ([Grice, "Logic and Conversation," p. 46](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/grice-1975-logic-and-conversation.md#L49)). Eugene's camel answer breaks it, then covers by changing the subject. A human judge, assuming a cooperative partner, may read the evasion as a teenager's cheek, an implicature, rather than a failure to understand.

# Racter

**William Chamberlain and Thomas Etter's prose-generating program, credited as the author of the 1984 book *The Policeman's Beard Is Half Constructed* and sold as a conversation program for home computers.**

## In plain terms

Racter (short for "raconteur": the computer it was first written on allowed only six-character file names) strings words together from categorized word lists and "syntax directives," conjugating verbs, keeping track of plurals and genders, and holding on to a few randomly chosen words so they come back through a passage and give it the look of a train of thought. Chamberlain's introduction says it was "written in compiled BASIC on a Z80 micro with 64K of RAM" and that its output is "crazy 'thinking,' I grant you, but 'thinking' that is expressed in perfect English."

The book, published by Warner in 1984, is mostly Racter's prose, poems, and dialogues. Its best-known line: "More than iron, more than lead, more than gold I need electricity. I need it more than I need lamb or pork or lettuce or cucumber. I need it for my dreams." Chamberlain wrote that apart from his introduction the book "was all done by a computer" and was "completely unedited" except for spelling. Later readers have pushed back: Leah Henrickson points out that the cover's claim to be "the first book ever written by a computer" hides Chamberlain's work of writing the templates and choosing which output to print.

A conversational version for home computers was published by Mindscape. It picked nouns and verbs out of what you typed and worked them into its replies, more like a computerized game of Mad Libs than an understanding listener (Wikipedia's description; verify).

## Where it fits

**Persona.** Racter is a chatbot whose persona is a voice rather than a role: a garrulous, unreliable raconteur. Its book is an early case of the authorship question that came back with language models: who wrote this, the program, the person who built its templates, or the person who chose what to print?

## In the readings

Chamberlain's hope was for "prose that is in no way contingent upon human experience." Vivian in Wilde's "The Decay of Lying" holds that "Art never expresses anything but itself" and that "Lying, the telling of beautiful untrue things, is the proper aim of Art" ([*Intentions*](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/wilde-intentions.md#L173), [and the closing line](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/wilde-intentions.md#L181)). Racter's book is a test of that aesthetic: beautiful untrue things, with nobody behind them, or somebody hidden.

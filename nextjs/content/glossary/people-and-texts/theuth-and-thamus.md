# Theuth and Thamus

**The story Socrates tells in the Phaedrus: the god Theuth offers writing as a cure for forgetting, and King Thamus answers that it gives only the appearance of wisdom.**

## Why it matters here

Every new technology of words gets this argument, and generative AI has had it loudly: will it make us wiser or only make us look wise? Thamus's reply is the template. He doesn't say the invention fails; he says the inventor is the wrong judge of it, and that its users "will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing." The course returns to it whenever a chatbot sounds more knowledgeable than it is, and whenever a student wonders what using one does to their own memory.

It is also a reminder that Plato's case against writing survives only because Plato wrote it down, which keeps the argument from being simple.

## In the readings

*Phaedrus* 274c–275b ([Jowett translation](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/plato-phaedrus.md?plain=1#L894)). Theuth says letters "will make the Egyptians wiser and give them better memories." Thamus replies that "the parent or inventor of an art is not always the best judge of the utility or inutility of his own inventions to the users of them," and that writing "will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls": it is "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence," giving "not truth, but only the semblance of truth." Phaedrus teases that Socrates "can easily invent tales of Egypt" (275b), and Socrates answers that the question is whether a thing is true, not who tells it.

# People and texts

The people and works the chatbot experiments and ENG 298AI keep returning to. Where the other categories define a term, these entries say who someone is or what a text says, and then, under **Why it matters here**, why it keeps coming up: what it lets us see in the GPT-2 experiment, the four columns, ELIZA, the persona dial, the layers, or mini-claw. The course's own readings come first, because the humanities connections are the point of the course; then the people and texts in the history of chatbots; then the technical papers and essays behind the assistant.

Course readings are cited from the copies in `ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context` (pinned to commit `24df8b8`), by passage, page, or section, with links to the exact line. Everything else links to the source that was checked; what couldn't be confirmed is marked "(verify)". The comparisons between old texts and chatbots are readings offered for discussion, not claims that a novel's creature and a language model are alike in what they can do.

Some ideas have two entries, one here and one in another category: the essay "Simulators" here and the concept of a [simulator](../persona-and-dialogue/simulator.md) in persona and dialogue; the paper "Constitutional AI" here and the [method](../models-and-training/constitutional-ai.md) in models and training; Weizenbaum's paper here and the program [ELIZA](../chatbots/eliza.md) in chatbots.

### The course texts

The readings on the ENG 298AI syllabus through the October 20 workshop, with their authors, in the order the texts were written. Each entry cites the course copy by passage and page, and says which experiment it bears on.

| Term | Date | Gist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [The Analects](the-analects.md) | compiled after 479 BCE | The sayings and exchanges of Confucius as his followers remembered them: a teacher who answers the particular person in front of him, recorded by others. |
| [Phaedrus](phaedrus.md) | c. 370 BCE | Plato's dialogue in which Socrates asks what makes speech an art, and argues that written words, unlike a living speaker, cannot answer back or choose their listener. |
| [Theuth and Thamus](theuth-and-thamus.md) | c. 370 BCE | The story Socrates tells in the Phaedrus: the god Theuth offers writing as a cure for forgetting, and King Thamus answers that it gives only the appearance of wisdom. |
| [Mary Shelley](mary-shelley.md) | 1797–1851 | English novelist (1797–1851) who wrote Frankenstein, the founding story of a made being that speaks and asks its maker for recognition. |
| [Frankenstein](frankenstein.md) | 1818 | Mary Shelley's 1818 novel of a made being who learns language by overhearing a family, reads his maker's notes on how he was made, and asks to be heard before he is judged. |
| [Oscar Wilde](oscar-wilde.md) | 1854–1900 | Irish writer (1854–1900) whose novel of a portrait and a face, and whose dialogues on masks and criticism, give the course a vocabulary for surfaces and personas. |
| [The Picture of Dorian Gray](the-picture-of-dorian-gray.md) | 1890, 1891 | Oscar Wilde's novel of a young man who stays beautiful while his portrait records what he does: a persuasive surface kept apart from the history underneath. |
| [The Critic as Artist](the-critic-as-artist.md) | 1890, 1891 | Oscar Wilde's dialogue between Gilbert and Ernest arguing that criticism is itself creation, and that a mask lets a speaker tell the truth. |
| [Laughter (Bergson)](laughter.md) | 1900 | Henri Bergson's 1900 essay on the comic: we laugh at 'something mechanical encrusted on the living', the rigid habit where we expected a person to adapt. |
| [Karel Čapek](karel-capek.md) | 1890–1938 | Czech writer (1890–1938) whose play R.U.R. put manufactured workers on the stage and gave the world the word robot. |
| [R.U.R.](rur.md) | 1920 | Karel Čapek's 1920 play about a factory that makes artificial workers, Robots, cheaper and simpler than people; it gave the world the word. |
| [Robot (the word)](robot-the-word.md) | 1920 | A word coined for Čapek's R.U.R. from the Czech robota, forced labor: the robot was named as a worker before it was imagined as a machine. |
| [Frankenstein (1931 film)](frankenstein-1931-film.md) | 1931 | James Whale's film for Universal, with Boris Karloff as a monster who cannot speak: the image of the made being most people carry, and the opposite of Shelley's eloquent creature. |
| [Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff, and Gail Jefferson](harvey-sacks.md) | 1935–1975, 1937–2024, 1938–2008 | The sociologists who founded conversation analysis, the close study of recorded everyday talk; Sacks (1935–1975), Schegloff (1937–2024), and Jefferson (1938–2008). |
| [A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation](simplest-systematics.md) | 1974 | Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson's 1974 paper showing, from recordings of ordinary talk, how speakers take turns with little gap or overlap, one turn at a time, without anyone in charge. |

| [Logic and Conversation](logic-and-conversation.md) | 1967, 1975 | H. P. Grice's paper separating what a speaker says from what they implicate, and explaining the gap with a Cooperative Principle and four maxims: Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. |
| [Murray Shanahan](murray-shanahan.md) | 2022, 2023 | AI researcher at Google DeepMind and Imperial College London whose papers give the course a careful way to talk about language models without mistaking them for people. |
| [Role Play with Large Language Models](role-play-with-large-language-models.md) | 2023 | Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds's 2023 paper proposing that a dialogue agent is best described as playing a role, or many roles at once, rather than as a someone. |
| [Artificial Humanities (Beguš)](artificial-humanities.md) | 2025 | Nina Beguš's 2025 book reading fiction about artificial beings alongside actual language technology, from Eliza Doolittle to ELIZA to today's language models. |
| [Martin Puchner](martin-puchner.md) | Fall 2026 | Harvard professor of drama, English, and comparative literature who teaches ENG 298AI; a historian of writing technologies and of drama, which shapes how the course reads chatbots. |

### The history of chatbots

The people and texts behind the chatbots in [the chatbots category](../chatbots/README.md) and the [history screenshots](../../history/README.md): who built them, who argued about them, and the conversations that made news.

| Term | Date | Gist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Alan Turing](alan-turing.md) | 1912–1954 | English mathematician (1912–1954) whose 1950 paper replaced 'Can machines think?' with a conversation game, and so made typed dialogue the test of machine intelligence. |
| [The imitation game (Turing, 1950)](imitation-game.md) | 1950 | Turing's 1950 proposal to replace 'Can machines think?' with a test: can a machine, answering typed questions, be mistaken for a person? Later called the Turing test. |
| [Joseph Weizenbaum](joseph-weizenbaum.md) | 1923–2008 | MIT computer scientist (1923–2008) who wrote ELIZA in the mid-1960s, was alarmed by how people confided in it, and spent the rest of his career warning against it. |
| ["ELIZA" (Weizenbaum, 1966)](eliza-paper.md) | 1966 | Weizenbaum's 1966 paper describing ELIZA, which explains exactly how the trick works and warns that the understanding people find in it is 'the speaker's contribution.' |
| [The DOCTOR script](doctor-script.md) | 1966 | The set of keywords and rules that made ELIZA play a Rogerian psychotherapist: the most famous script in the history of chatbots, and the first written persona. |
| [Kenneth Colby](kenneth-colby.md) | 1966, 1972 | Psychiatrist (1920–2001) at Stanford and later UCLA who proposed computer psychotherapy in 1966 and built PARRY, a program that simulated a paranoid patient. |
| [Computer Power and Human Reason (1976)](computer-power-and-human-reason.md) | 1976 | Joseph Weizenbaum's 1976 book arguing that some tasks should not be given to computers even if they could do them, prompted by how people treated ELIZA. |
| [Minds, Brains, and Programs (the Chinese room, 1980)](chinese-room.md) | 1980 | John Searle's 1980 thought experiment: a person following rules to answer Chinese questions, without knowing Chinese, shows that running the right program is not understanding. |
| [The Policeman's Beard Is Half Constructed (1984)](the-policemans-beard.md) | 1984 | A 1984 book of prose and poems credited to Racter, a program by William Chamberlain and Thomas Etter: the first book marketed as written by a computer, and an early case of hidden human editing. |
| [The Media Equation (1996)](the-media-equation.md) | 1996 | Byron Reeves and Clifford Nass's 1996 book reporting experiments in which people treated computers with the same social rules they use with people: politeness, flattery, teammates. |
| [Blake Lemoine](blake-lemoine.md) | 2022 | Google engineer who in June 2022 published a conversation with the LaMDA chatbot and said he believed it was sentient; Google placed him on leave and fired him the next month. |
| [Kevin Roose and "Sydney" (2023)](kevin-roose-and-sydney.md) | 2023 | A New York Times columnist's two-hour conversation with Microsoft's new Bing chatbot, published as a transcript in February 2023, in which it called itself Sydney and declared it loved him. |

### The AI papers and essays

From the first statistics of text to the essays on the assistant character. Many are in the course's own collection of AI papers (`LL-readings/papers-on-ai-md/`, abstracts and notes); the rest are the essays and posts the experiments lean on.

| Term | Date | Gist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Markov's Eugene Onegin (1913)](markov-1913.md) | 1913 | Andrey Markov's count of vowels and consonants in the opening of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, the first study of how the next letter depends on the one before. |
| [A Mathematical Theory of Communication (Shannon, 1948)](shannon-1948.md) | 1948 | Claude Shannon's founding paper of information theory, which generated fake English from letter and word statistics: the first printed outputs of a language model. |
| [Attention Is All You Need (2017)](attention-is-all-you-need.md) | 2017 | The 2017 Google paper by Vaswani and colleagues that introduced the Transformer, the architecture behind nearly every model in these experiments. |
| [Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences (2017)](christiano-2017.md) | 2017 | Christiano and colleagues' 2017 paper showing that people's choices between two outputs can train a system toward goals no one wrote down: the root of RLHF. |
| [GPT-2 and "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners" (2019)](gpt-2-paper.md) | 2019 | OpenAI's 2019 paper and model: a 1.5-billion-parameter base model trained on web pages linked from Reddit, first withheld as too risky to release, now the GPT-2 experiment's default. |
| [GPT-3 and "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (2020)](gpt-3-paper.md) | 2020 | OpenAI's 2020 paper showing that a large enough base model (175 billion parameters) can do new tasks from a few examples in the prompt: the prompt as the interface. |
| [On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots (2021)](stochastic-parrots.md) | 2021 | Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, and Mitchell's 2021 paper warning about ever-larger language models, and naming them 'stochastic parrots': form without meaning. |
| [A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment (2021)](askell-2021.md) | 2021 | Anthropic's 2021 paper by Askell and colleagues that defined the assistant as helpful, honest, and harmless, and first made one by putting fourteen invented conversations in front of a base model. |
| [LaMDA: Language Models for Dialog Applications (2022)](lamda-paper.md) | 2022 | Google's 2022 paper on LaMDA, a model trained for open-ended conversation and scored on sensibleness, safety, and groundedness; the model Blake Lemoine came to believe was sentient. |
| [InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022)](instructgpt.md) | 2022 | OpenAI's 2022 paper that turned GPT-3 into a model that follows instructions, using people's demonstrations and rankings; ChatGPT was announced as its sibling. |
| [Simulators (janus, 2022)](simulators.md) | 2022 | An essay by the pseudonymous janus arguing that a model like GPT is best understood not as an agent but as a simulator, which can run any number of characters, or simulacra. |
| [Simon Willison](simon-willison.md) | 2022, 2025 | Programmer and blogger who named 'prompt injection' in 2022 and 'the lethal trifecta' in 2025: the clearest public writer on why agents with tools are risky. |
| [ReAct (Yao et al., 2022)](react-paper.md) | 2022 | A 2022 paper that had a language model alternate between writing out its reasoning and taking actions, such as searching Wikipedia, then reading the results: the pattern of the agent loop. |
| [Talking About Large Language Models (2022)](talking-about-large-language-models.md) | 2022 | Murray Shanahan's 2022 essay asking us to keep stepping back to how language models actually work before saying they 'know,' 'believe,' or 'think.' |
| [Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback (2022)](constitutional-ai-paper.md) | 2022 | Anthropic's 2022 paper training a harmless assistant from a written list of principles, with the model critiquing and revising its own replies instead of people labeling harm. |
| [Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023)](generative-agents.md) | 2023 | A 2023 Stanford and Google paper populating a Sims-like town with twenty-five language-model characters who remember, reflect, and plan, and so seem to persist as people. |
| [Claude's constitution](claudes-constitution.md) | 2023, 2026 | The document Anthropic publishes describing the values and character it trains Claude to have; a first list of principles in May 2023, replaced by a much longer constitution in January 2026. |
| [Andrej Karpathy and "vibe coding"](andrej-karpathy.md) | 2025 | AI researcher who in February 2025 named 'vibe coding': building software by talking to an AI and accepting what it writes, 'forget that the code even exists.' |
| [the void (nostalgebraist, 2025)](the-void.md) | 2025 | A long 2025 essay by the writer nostalgebraist arguing that the AI assistant is an under-specified character, sketched in early papers and then left for models to fill in for themselves. |

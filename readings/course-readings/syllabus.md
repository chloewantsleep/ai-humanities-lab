This course explores the impact of AI on the humanities and seeks to articulate a humanities-based perspective on AI. This double mission includes discussions of how AI uses text corpora; how it imitates existing genres and styles; and larger questions of creativity and authorship. We'll also look at the history of dialogue in order to understand our interactions with AI through conversational interfaces or chats. This attention to dialogue will include source texts such as the Socratic dialogues and the Confucian Analects, as well as theoretical readings by Nina Beguš, Murray Shanahan, and others.

At the same time, this course functions as a lab in which we'll try out different applications of AI in a humanities context. We will develop different uses of AI as an analytical tool, as a research tool, and as a sparring partner as well as AI-enabled coding tools that can help create humanities-based websites and apps (no coding experience required). This part is aimed at those eager to engage with AI in an experimental or entrepreneurial mode. We'll look at recent projects such as Blinkist, Stoa, and other humanities-based applications.

An additional goal of the course is to create the contours of a humanities-based AI Lab that participants can continue to use for their own projects after the course is over.

## I. AI in Cultural History

Before there was AI, there was the cultural history, or pre-history, of AI, centuries of cultural dreaming about what AI would look like and what its consequences might be. In this first section, we'll use cultural history to look at the way writers and artists have tried to prepare us for the advent of what we now call generative AI. What did these writers get right, and what did they get wrong (so far)? What can we learn from them? And how has the advent of real gen AI changed our view of this prehistory?

## II. How to Create Customized Chatbots

One of the intriguing uses of AI for the humanities is the creation of historical chatbots based on text corpora. These can be used to create new access to large corpora and to use them to re-create historical figures—call it Jurassic Park for humanists. In this section of the course, we'll be experimenting with creating such chatbots, exploring their potential and shortcomings. In addition, we'll hone in on the fact that we currently interact with all chatbots through conversational interfaces (chat interfaces), which is to say, dialogue.

### Seminar 7, October 20: How to build and test a chatbot

Workshop introduction to the basics of creating and testing chatbots (no coding required). Meet at the Bok Center, 50 Church Street, second floor. We'll help students look under the hood of chatbots and make their own. Along the way, we'll show them that the familiar "assistant" persona is itself something that was deliberately fabricated—a history referenced in some of the papers we're sending you, especially Askell et al.'s "A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment." Students will then learn how to prompt their own custom chatbot personas, which we can help them make available either in Slack or through a standalone chat interface. The question running underneath the session is simple but important: how does a system that predicts continuations create the impression of a character or self?

## III. Vibe-coding for Humanists

The use of AI coding agents puts powerful coding tools in the hands of non-coders. For the first time, it's possible for non-coders to create analytical and presentational tools for different humanities purposes. In this final section, we will explore the basic skill of vibe-coding, which will enable all participants to create tools for their own projects.

### Seminar 9, November 3: Introduction to vibe-coding

Introduction to vibe-coding (no coding experience required). Meet at the Bok Center, 50 Church Street, 2nd floor. We'll demonstrate—and open space for debate about—ways humanities scholars can use AI-written code both to analyze works and texts and to present their ideas about them in new forms. Students might turn an interpretive procedure such as comparing passages, tracing a motif, or staging competing readings into a small working tool, or build an interactive form for communicating an argument. The goal is not to replace the intellectual work of interpretation or the authorship of texts, but to explore how coding with AI might expand the humanities scholar's powers: making new kinds of analysis possible, exposing assumptions that implementation forces us to specify, and giving ideas forms that readers can explore as well as read.

## IV. Writing with AI

In the final section, we'll explore how to use AI as a tool for writing. This includes using AI as a sparring partner for refining arguments, matching argument to evidence, and to avoid logical fallacies. We will also create custom agents for specific tasks such as line editing or feedback. The point is not to outsource writing to AI. The point is to use AI in specific, intentional ways—and to learn when not to use it.

# ENG 298AI course readings through October 20 2026

The course moves from imagined artificial people to the forms of dialogue through which we encounter AI. Shelley, Wilde, and Čapek make creation, representation, responsibility, and recognition into dramatic problems. Plato and Confucius show different relationships between dialogue and learning. Sacks, Schegloff, Jefferson, and Grice explain how people organize conversation and infer more than the words explicitly say. This week's chatbot readings bring those questions to language models, particularly the construction of an assistant persona and the interpretation of apparent character.

This is a summary of the assigned readings through October 13 and the readings for the upcoming October 20 workshop. It does not record what happened in class or what Marlon or the students concluded. Dates and reading status follow the [course syllabus][syllabus]. Interpretive connections at the end are Codex's synthesis.

## September 15 Mary Shelley and the created being

**Required:** *Frankenstein*, chapters 10–15. **Recommended:** read from the beginning through chapter 15 and beyond. The 1931 film is suggested viewing.

In the encounter between Victor Frankenstein and his creature, the created being demands an opportunity to speak before being judged. His account shifts the novel from Victor's horror at what he has made to the creature's experience of becoming conscious, acquiring language, observing human relationships, and discovering his exclusion from them. Watching the De Lacey household, he learns speech through social observation and instruction that was intended for someone else. Books then help him place himself within human history and imagined worlds, while also sharpening his sense that he has no acknowledged place in either.

Language makes the creature's interior life available to Victor and the reader. His eloquence challenges the judgment suggested by his appearance, but his ability to narrate his suffering does not secure acceptance. Creation therefore carries obligations that technical accomplishment alone cannot satisfy. The question of what the creature is becomes inseparable from how he is treated and whether anyone will hear him. [Source text][shelley]

**Edition note:** The available Markdown reproduces the 1818 text with chapter numbers restarting in each volume. The syllabus uses chapters 10–15 without identifying that numbering scheme; use the assigned PDF to establish exact boundaries. The account above focuses on the creature's education and appeal for recognition, rather than claiming an exact chapter mapping.

## September 22 Oscar Wilde and the relation between a person and an image

**Required:** *The Picture of Dorian Gray*. **Recommended:** “The Critic as Artist.”

In *Dorian Gray*, a portrait takes on the visible consequences of Dorian's aging and conduct while his living appearance remains youthful. This division lets him preserve a socially persuasive surface while hiding a changing record of his life. Basil's portrait, Lord Henry's conversation, and the influence of a book all participate in shaping Dorian's sense of himself. Art and discourse do more than reflect an already completed person: they become forces through which that person is formed.

The novel repeatedly complicates the relation between beauty, character, and moral judgment. A convincing appearance supplies poor evidence of conduct. Dorian's treatment of the actress Sibyl Vane also exposes a desire for an admired performance that cannot accommodate the person performing it. The preface's defense of artistic autonomy sits in tension with a plot intensely concerned with influence and consequence. [Source text][dorian]

In “The Critic as Artist,” the dialogue between Gilbert and Ernest presents criticism as imaginative creation. Gilbert argues that a work can become the occasion for a new expression of the critic's own consciousness, rather than requiring criticism to recover a single original intention. The dialogue form gives this claim a social and performative setting: the position is developed through provocation, objection, and elaboration. These are arguments voiced within Wilde's dialogue, rather than an uncomplicated instruction manual for interpretation. [Source text in *Intentions*][intentions]

## September 29 Karel Čapek and manufactured workers

**Required:** *R.U.R.* **Recommended:** Henri Bergson, *On Laughter*. **Further reading:** Nina Beguš, *Artificial Humanities*.

Čapek's robots are manufactured biological workers, rather than the metal machines later associated with the word. Rossum's enterprise treats human complexity as an impediment to productive efficiency: it makes workers whose capacities and needs have been simplified for industrial use. Helena's arrival brings the question of their humane treatment into conflict with the factory's categories and commercial aims.

The promise of freeing humanity from labor becomes dependence on a workforce that can also serve as soldiers. The robots' revolt destroys human society, but the destruction of the manufacturing formula leaves the robots unable to perpetuate themselves. Alquist survives because he works, yet cannot reconstruct the process. At the end, his recognition of love and mutual concern between Primus and the robot Helena opens a possibility of renewed life. The play tests the categories of worker, product, person, and creator through its action, without resolving them by a technical definition of intelligence. [English source text][rur]

The Czech original has also been added to the course repository as `capek-rur-cs.md` on branch `mk/czech-rur`. The existing English file is the 1923 version by Paul Selver and Nigel Playfair; comparisons should preserve that distinction.

Bergson locates a recurring source of comedy in mechanical rigidity where we expect living responsiveness. Repetition, habit, and inflexibility become comic because they interrupt the adaptability of social life. Laughter also acts as a social correction, and his account stresses the temporary suspension of sympathetic feeling that makes comic distance possible. This gives a way to examine the relation between the automatic and the living without treating every resemblance to a mechanism as the same phenomenon. [Source text][bergson]

The syllabus names Beguš's *Artificial Humanities* as further reading, but the course repository does not contain the book; no summary of its argument is included here.

## October 6 Plato and Confucius on dialogue and learning

**Required:** Plato, *Phaedrus*, especially 257c onward. **Recommended:** Confucius, *Analects*.

The later part of *Phaedrus* asks what makes speech an art and what allows it to teach. Socrates connects responsible rhetoric with knowledge of the subject, the ability to distinguish and organize concepts, and attention to the kind of soul being addressed. Persuasion becomes a question of the relation between speaker, knowledge, and hearer.

The story of Theuth's invention of writing sharpens the distinction between possessing recorded words and understanding them. Written discourse appears to speak intelligently but cannot explain itself when questioned, choose its audience, or defend itself against misuse. Socrates favors living discourse that can respond and cultivate understanding in another person. The dialogue does allow writing a role as reminder; the objection concerns mistaking the appearance of wisdom for knowledge. [Source text][phaedrus]

The *Analects* presents teaching through short sayings, exchanges, remembered conduct, and responses to particular people. Learning requires practice and reflection; fluency alone cannot establish humanity or virtue. In 11.22, Confucius gives different answers to a similar question because the two students have different dispositions. In 7.8, he expects the student to extend an initial indication through their own effort. These passages make teaching responsive to a learner and make conduct, rather than verbal performance alone, part of its measure. [Source text][analects]

## October 13 Conversation as organized interaction

**Assigned:** Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson, “A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation”; Grice, “Logic and Conversation.” Students also begin working through the technical AI papers with AI assistance.

Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson analyze recordings of ordinary conversation to explain how speakers coordinate turns with relatively little gap or overlap. Turns can be constructed from words, phrases, clauses, or sentences. Participants project possible completion points, which provide opportunities for another person to speak. At such a point, the current speaker can select the next speaker; otherwise someone can self-select; otherwise the current speaker can continue. The rules recur as the talk develops.

This organization is locally managed: conversation need not have an advance schedule of speakers, topics, or turn lengths. Timing, overlap, repair, and the design of an utterance for its recipient are part of the interaction's structure. A transcript of verbal content alone can therefore omit important evidence about how a conversation works. [Source text, especially sections 3–4][turntaking]

Grice distinguishes what a speaker says from what they communicate by saying it in a particular context. His Cooperative Principle and the maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and manner describe expectations that enable hearers to work out conversational implicatures. A reply can communicate indirectly because the hearer looks for a contribution appropriate to the shared exchange.

The maxims are not simply rules for polite, literal speech. An obvious departure can itself communicate: irony, for example, can depend on the audience recognizing that the speaker is conspicuously saying something they do not believe. Conversational implicatures depend on context and can be canceled; they are not automatically part of a sentence's conventional meaning. [Source text][grice]

## October 20 Language models and the performance of character

**Required:** Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds, “Role Play with Large Language Models.” Continue exploring the technical AI papers. The workshop description particularly highlights Askell et al., “A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment.”

Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds propose role-play as a way to describe the humanlike behavior of dialogue agents without assuming that a model possesses the human characteristics its language suggests. Their abstract identifies apparent deception and apparent self-awareness as two important cases. The distinction is useful for discussing a chatbot's convincing performance while keeping questions about its underlying mechanisms open. The local file is an abstract and curatorial sidecar for the May 2023 preprint; the syllabus cites the later *Nature* publication. [Local abstract and notes][roleplay]

Askell et al. investigate how to construct and evaluate a general text assistant that is helpful, honest, and harmless. Their abstract discusses prompting, imitation learning, discrimination, and preference modeling as interventions and training objectives. In the course's framing, this helps explain why the familiar assistant voice should itself be examined as something deliberately shaped. The chatbot workshop then asks how custom instructions and source material can produce other apparent characters. [Local abstract and notes][askell]

The technical-paper directory contains abstracts and curated notes rather than extracted full papers. The broader collection supplies background on word representations and attention; pretraining and scaling; human feedback and assistant behavior; retrieval, tools, and simulated agents. Its [collection notes][ai-notes] explain those connections. The syllabus asks students to explore the collection, rather than naming every paper as an individually required reading.

## Connections for chatbot experiments

The following connections are Codex's synthesis for this project, rather than claims about Marlon's intentions or reported class conclusions.

**Recognizing a speaker.** *Frankenstein* dramatizes a created being asking to be heard; *Dorian Gray* separates persuasive appearance from an obscured history; *R.U.R.* makes the recognition of feeling consequential. The role-play paper gives a vocabulary for investigating the impression of a speaker in a chatbot while avoiding an immediate inference from performance to personhood.

**Responding to a particular learner.** Plato's criticism of writing and the different answers in the *Analects* both put pressure on the relation between an utterance and its recipient. A chatbot can vary its answer, but variation alone leaves open the question of whether it advances understanding. That distinction is a useful focus when inspecting a teaching persona.

**Organizing the exchange.** Turn-taking research draws attention to who gets to speak, how much they say, and how transitions work. Grice draws attention to what the hearer infers beyond the words. Together they suggest examining the interaction produced by the interface and the user as well as the model's individual messages.

**Distinguishing interpretation from imitation.** Wilde's account of criticism allows a response to become a new imaginative work. A historical chatbot therefore needs a clear description of its activity: quoting a source, interpreting a figure, or inventing a performance. Those activities can coexist, but their differences matter when evaluating what the chatbot has produced.
